Source-based retellings; original applications are not scripture quotations.

Kisa Gotami searches for a house untouched by death
Source: Dhammapada Commentary — Book VIII, story 13, associated with verse 114
Type: Commentarial narrative
Situation: I feel that nobody understands what losing this person is like.
What happened: In the commentary’s story, Kisa Gotami seeks help after her child dies. She is asked to bring mustard seed from a home where no one has died. Visiting homes reveals that other families have also known loss.
Teaching: The story moves from isolated grief toward recognition of shared mortality. It belongs to commentary, not the wording of Dhammapada verse 114 itself.
Why it fits: This is specifically about death and the loneliness of grief.
Application: Knowing that grief is shared need not make your particular loss smaller. It may give you someone beside whom you can carry it.
Try: Look for one person or group where you can speak about the person you lost without being rushed.

Angulimala stops causing harm
Source: Majjhima Nikaya — MN 86, Angulimala Sutta
Type: Scripture narrative
Situation: I want to change but feel defined by what I did before.
What happened: Angulimala has caused grave harm. After meeting the Buddha, he abandons violence and enters a different way of life. The sutta also describes consequences he still faces; change does not erase the past.
Teaching: The narrative distinguishes stopping harm from escaping responsibility.
Why it fits: This fits responsibility for your own past conduct, not guilt imposed on you unfairly.
Application: Your situation may be very different. The relevant lesson is to make change visible through conduct and repair, not to ask others to forget what happened.
Try: Choose one harmful pattern to stop and one repair you can begin safely.

The Buddha does not return an insult
Source: Samyutta Nikaya — SN 7.2, Akkosa Sutta
Type: Teaching dialogue
Situation: Someone insults me and I feel pulled into fighting back.
What happened: A brahmin angrily abuses the Buddha. The Buddha asks what happens to offered food when a guest does not accept it, and uses that comparison to explain why he does not join the exchange of abuse.
Teaching: The dialogue shows refusal to add another round of anger.
Why it fits: This fits provocation and an escalating argument, not a requirement to tolerate abuse.
Application: You can decline an insult’s invitation to fight while still protecting your boundary. Silence is not the only option; a brief limit or leaving can also be a choice.
Try: Prepare one sentence such as: I will discuss the issue, but not while I am being insulted.
