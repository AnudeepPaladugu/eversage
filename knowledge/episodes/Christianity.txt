Source-based retellings; original applications are not scripture quotations.

Jesus speaks honestly about fear
Source: Bible — Mark 14:32–42
Type: Scripture narrative
Situation: I am frightened by something difficult I have to face.
What happened: In Gethsemane, Jesus tells his disciples that he is deeply distressed. He asks them to stay near while he prays about what lies ahead. The scene includes honest fear, a wish for companionship and continued prayer.
Teaching: In this passage, deep distress and faith are present together.
Why it fits: The connection is fear before a difficult event, not a promise that the event will be removed.
Application: You do not have to pretend to be calm before asking for help. Your circumstances differ from the Passion, but honest words and chosen support can help you face the next part.
Try: Tell one trusted person what you are afraid of and ask for a specific kind of support.

Peter is given responsibility after failure
Source: Bible — John 18:15–27; 21:15–19
Type: Scripture narrative
Situation: I made a mistake and feel I cannot begin again.
What happened: Peter denies knowing Jesus during the arrest and trial. After the resurrection, Jesus speaks with Peter, asks about his love and gives him responsibility to care for his followers. Peter’s failure is not the last scene of the relationship.
Teaching: Restoration in the story includes renewed responsibility, not pretending that nothing happened.
Why it fits: This fits regret about your own actions, not blame for harm someone else caused you.
Application: A mistake can call for repair and changed behaviour without becoming your permanent identity.
Try: Name one repair you can make safely and honestly. Commit to one action that shows what you will do differently.

Jesus weeps with those who are grieving
Source: Bible — John 11:17–36
Type: Scripture narrative
Situation: Someone I love has died and I feel pressure to be strong.
What happened: Jesus meets Martha and Mary after Lazarus has died. He speaks with them, sees their grief and weeps. This happens before the later events of the chapter; the grief is not skipped.
Teaching: The scene gives sorrow a place within faith and friendship.
Why it fits: This is about mourning a death, not ordinary disappointment.
Application: You do not need to prove strength by hiding grief. The later miracle in the narrative is not a promise that a reader’s loss will be reversed.
Try: Choose someone who can listen without rushing you. Tell them whether you want company, practical help or quiet.

Help comes from an unexpected neighbour
Source: Bible — Luke 10:25–37
Type: Parable told by Jesus
Situation: I want to help someone but others say they are not one of us.
What happened: In Jesus’ parable, an injured traveller is passed by before a Samaritan stops. The Samaritan tends to him and arranges continued care. The answer to the question about a neighbour is expressed through practical mercy.
Teaching: The parable places the person’s need ahead of an expected social boundary.
Why it fits: This fits a decision about practical help and prejudice; it is a parable, not an event Jesus claims happened to him.
Application: When it is safe to help, a useful action may matter more than deciding whether someone belongs to your group.
Try: Ask what help is actually needed and what you can safely offer, rather than making a promise you cannot keep.

The younger son returns after his choices fail
Source: Bible — Luke 15:11–32
Type: Parable told by Jesus
Situation: I want to return to my family after making mistakes.
What happened: The younger son leaves home, spends his inheritance and reaches a point of need. He prepares to admit what he has done and returns. His father welcomes him, while the elder brother’s hurt also becomes part of the conversation.
Teaching: The parable includes return, confession, welcome and a second family member’s unresolved feelings.
Why it fits: This fits returning after your own mistake, not an obligation to return to an unsafe home.
Application: Repair may begin with an honest approach, while leaving room for the other person’s feelings. Real families are not required to respond exactly like the father in the parable.
Try: Prepare a short apology that names your action and the repair you can offer, without demanding an immediate welcome.
